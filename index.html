Annotation · How this page works

I built this page as a scrollytelling case file in seven movements. It opens with my personal prologue, moves through four historical cases (I Rome, II Britain, III Alexandria, IV London), ties them together with the red string, opens the sealed Case V (the US dollar) and its continuation on nominal and real spending (concern, and hope), sums every case up in the verdict, and closes with my epilogue.

I gave every case the same three beats so you only have to learn the pattern once: (1) a sticky chart that changes as the paper notes scroll past it, (2) a full-width breakout visual that restates the case at a glance, and (3) a paper summary folder with the headline figures. I don't name a culture until its case opens, and I hold the dollar back until the end so the macro point lands as the reveal.

  • ColourI use silver to verdigris as my data language: the lighter the coin, the purer the metal; bronze and green mean it is failing. Bronze, brass, file paper and the red string carry my story. I put these notes in slate so they sit outside both palettes.
  • TypeI chose Cinzel for titles because it echoes Roman inscriptions, Special Elite for evidence tags and chart labels as my investigator's typewriter, and Crimson Pro for the narrative.
  • CopyMy narrative paragraphs are still placeholder text made of the letter a while I draft them. The headings, dates, chart labels, figures and sources are real.
  • ChartsEvery sticky chart has a hover layer: move or drag across it to scrub through the records, and the coin in the corner repaints to match.
  • ReadingUse the ✎ button at the bottom right to hide my notes for a clean read.

Annotation · Case III · Alexandria · scrolling chart

I plot Ptolemaic bronze types as a scatter: face value across, mean weight up, dot size by specimens weighed. The slope of each line from the origin is grams of bronze per unit of value, so a flatter line means a debased standard.

In note III-c I animate one coin, Sv1424B, sliding from 100 to 120 units at the same 22.5 g. I wanted you to watch debasement by decree, with no new metal struck.

Annotation · Case V continued · nominal and real spending

I bring in the second half of my US file: spending in nominal dollars and the same series in 1913 dollars (nominal × 1913 CPI ÷ that year's CPI). Both lines share one axis in billions, so there's no dual-axis trick. The gap between them is what prices took.

The five notes alternate on purpose. Concern: the headline number is mostly prices, and real spending fell three years running in 2022-24. Hope: on a log scale the real line climbs 22-fold, about 2.8% a year, and it turned up again in 2025.

  • MotionI animate the y-axis from linear to log, then zoom the x-axis into 2000-2025 and back out.
  • InteractionHover or drag to scrub any year for nominal, real, inflation and the real change.
  • DataMacro_US_Data.xlsx, columns "Nominal $'s in Billions" and "Real $'s in Billions 1913 Dollars".

Annotation · The verdict · final summary

This replaces the comparison I cut from note V-e. Instead of forcing five measures onto one timeline, I measure every case against its own starting value (= 100%) and show three moments: the start, the lowest record and the last record. The red string runs from start to low; a silver bracket shows any recovery. I keep hope in fine silver, the "pure" end of my metal scale, so it never borrows verdigris, which means corrosion here.

The last row is the reason for hope from Case V: real spending, which is not a coin at all. It runs off the scale at 2,187% of its 1913 level, and I draw it that way on purpose.
